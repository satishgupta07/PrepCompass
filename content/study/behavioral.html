Behavioral & Leadership: my handwritten notebook

The managerial and behavioural round for senior frontend and full-stack roles: how to structure answers, the questions that keep coming back, and fill-in-the-blank scripts for your own stories

Every sample answer here is a template with [blanks]. The numbers and situations marked example are illustrations, not facts about you. Replace each one with something that really happened. Interviewers probe the details, so an invented story falls apart in follow-up questions.

How to use these notes

  • Start with page 2 and build your story bank first: 6 to 8 real stories. Almost every question on later pages is answered by re-using one of them.
  • For each question, read the Say this skeleton, then write your own version in the blanks, then say it aloud with a timer: about two minutes.
  • Always include what you did (“I”, not “we”), a number for the result, and what you learned or would change.
  • Record yourself once. It is the fastest way to find filler words and rambling.
  • Do the Quick check on the last page, answers are folded away.

What interviewers are really scoringread this before anything else

Say this“For a senior role the question behind every question is: can I trust this person to own an outcome, work well with others, and raise the people around them? The stories are just evidence.”
SignalWhat a strong answer showsTypical questions
Ownershipyou took responsibility beyond your ticket and followed it to the result“a feature you owned”, “a time you went beyond your role”
Judgement under pressureyou prioritised, cut scope deliberately, and communicated trade-offs“tight deadline”, “conflicting priorities”
Collaborationyou worked with design, backend, QA and product, and disagreed without damage“disagreement with a designer or backend developer”
Communicationyou adapt the message to the audience, and you explain numbers in business terms“explain performance to non-technical stakeholders”
Quality and reliabilityyou prevent, detect, respond and learn“a production bug”, “a build that broke the UI”
Leadershipyou mentor, set standards, make decisions visible and reduce risk“mentoring juniors”, “technical decision-making”
Growth mindsetyou name a real mistake, what it cost, and what changed“a failure”, “how do you keep learning”

STAR, with a Result and a Lessonthe structure for every story

Say this“I answer in four beats: Situation in two sentences, Task (what was mine), Action (what I did, in order, with the reasoning), Result (a number), then one line on what I learned.”
BeatShare of the answerDoDo not
Situation10%team, product, scale, why it matteredgive a long backstory
Task10%the goal and your specific responsibilityblur the team’s job with yours
Action50%2 to 4 concrete steps; the decision you made and why; who you involvedsay “we” for everything, or list tools without reasons
Result20%a measurable outcome (time, errors, conversion, satisfaction), and the business effectend with “and it went well”
Lesson10%what you would do differently or now do by defaultpretend nothing could improve
  • Length: about two minutes (roughly 300 words). Stop and let them ask follow-ups.
  • “I” not “we”: they are hiring you. Credit the team once, then describe your part.
  • Numbers beat adjectives: “cut p75 LCP from 4.1 s to 2.3 s” beats “made it much faster”.
  • Real beats impressive: a modest true story you can discuss for ten minutes beats a dramatic one you cannot.
⚠ weak answers“We had a deadline and we all worked really hard and it launched.” No decision, no personal action, no number, no lesson. Also avoid blaming a named colleague, saying you have no weaknesses, and answering with hypotheticals (“I would…”) when they asked for a past example (“tell me about a time…”).

Remember!

  • S-T-A-R-L, half the time on Action
  • “I”, one number, one lesson
  • About 2 minutes, then stop
  • Past example, not hypothetical

One story answers many questionsthe highest-leverage preparation

Say this“I do not memorise forty answers. I prepare six to eight real stories and map each to several questions. A single incident story can answer ‘production bug’, ‘working under pressure’, ‘ownership’ and ‘a mistake’, just with a different emphasis.”

  • If you lack a story for a category, borrow from a side project, an open-source contribution, a hackathon or a college project, and say so. Honest and specific is fine.
  • Collect the numbers now: dashboards, release notes, old tickets, performance reports. Memory fades; the numbers make the story credible.

  • 6 to 8 stories, mapped to many questions
  • Find the numbers before the interview
  • Change the spotlight, keep the facts

“Tell me about delivering a feature under a tight deadline”asked in 5+ loops

Say this“I first agree what must ship versus what can wait, in writing, with the product owner. Then I cut scope rather than quality, de-risk the hardest part first, and communicate progress daily, so the deadline is never a surprise.”
sample answer (template)

S: “At [company] we had [N weeks] to ship [feature] before [event: a launch, a contract date]. The team was [size] and [a constraint: a dependency, a missing API].”

T: “I owned the frontend delivery of [the part].”

A: “First I sat with the product owner and split the work into must-have, should-have and nice-to-have, and we agreed to launch with [the must-haves] and a simple [fallback]. Second, I tackled the riskiest unknown first, [the integration / the data shape], with a thin end-to-end slice by [day], so surprises came early. Third, I put the unfinished parts behind a feature flag so we could ship safely, and I posted a short daily update: done, next, blocked.”

R: “We shipped on [date] with [the core flows]; [metric: error rate, adoption]. The deferred items went out in [the next sprint]. [Optional: the stakeholder’s reaction.]”

L: “I now ask for the must-have list on day one, and I raise risk the moment I see it, not at the deadline.”

  • Show prioritisation logic: impact versus effort, risk, and dependencies.
  • Never trade away silently: tests, accessibility and security are not “scope” to cut. Say what you protected.
  • Follow-up: “What if they refused to cut scope?” → show the options (more people rarely helps late, move the date, reduce quality knowingly with a payback plan) and who decides.

“Tell me about a production bug, or a release that broke the UI”asked in 5+ loops

Say this“Order matters: stop the bleeding first (roll back or flag off), communicate, find the root cause, fix and verify, then prevent a repeat with a blameless post-mortem. Restoring users comes before understanding why.”
PhaseWhat I doWhat I say
1 · Detect and triageconfirm scope: who, how many, which browsers or flows; check release time, errors, vitals“Error rate on [flow] jumped at [time], right after release [version].”
2 · Mitigateroll back, flag off, or hotfix, whichever is fastest and safest“I rolled back first; root cause came second.”
3 · Communicateone clear channel, regular updates, plain language, an owner for each thread“Support and the manager had updates every [30 min].”
4 · Fix and verifyreproduce, fix, test, release gradually, watch the metrics“The cause was [X]; I added a regression test.”
5 · Preventblameless post-mortem: timeline, root cause, 2 to 3 concrete actions with owners“We added [a canary, a test, an alert] so it cannot recur silently.”
sample answer (template)

S: “After our Friday release of [feature], [N]% of users on [browser/device] could not [complete checkout / see the page]. It affected [revenue / users].”

A: “Our alert fired at [time]. I confirmed from the error tracker that it matched the release, and we rolled back within [N] minutes. I posted updates in the incident channel. After that I reproduced it: [root cause, e.g. a polyfill missing in an older Safari / an undefined value from a changed API field]. I fixed it, added a regression test and a browser to the test matrix, and re-released behind a flag at [1%] first.”

R: “Total impact was [N] minutes. We then added [canary release and an error-rate alert], and similar issues were caught in staging [twice] in the next quarter.”

L: “I treat ‘how did this reach production?’ as the real bug, not just the line of code.”

⚠ weak answerBlaming a teammate, or the QA team; skipping the rollback and describing only the debugging; or claiming nothing like this ever happened to you. A calm, structured response to a real failure is the thing being scored.

Remember!

  • Deadlines: agree must-haves, cut scope not quality
  • Incidents: mitigate → communicate → fix → prevent
  • Blameless, with 2 to 3 concrete actions

“How do you handle a disagreement with a designer or backend developer?”asked in 4 loops

Say this“I assume good intent, get specific about the shared goal, bring data or a prototype instead of opinions, and look for an option that satisfies the underlying need. If we still disagree, we escalate the decision to the owner, and then I commit fully.”
StepExample move
1 · Understand their reason“Help me understand what you are optimising for.” Often the real constraint is hidden (a deadline, an API limit, brand rules).
2 · State the shared goal“We both want [fast checkout / a consistent UI].”
3 · Bring evidencea quick prototype, a performance measurement, a user-research finding, an a11y audit
4 · Offer options with trade-offsA: [design as drawn, costs X]. B: [close variant, costs less, keeps the intent].
5 · Decide and committhe owner decides (design owns look, backend owns the data contract); document it; disagree and commit
sample answer (template)

S: “The designer wanted [a heavy animated hero / a custom data table], and I was concerned about [performance / accessibility / delivery time].”

A: “Instead of arguing, I asked what the animation was meant to achieve, and it was [draw attention to the offer]. I built a quick prototype and measured: it added [N] KB and pushed LCP to [X] s on a mid-range phone. I showed both of us the numbers and proposed [a lighter version with CSS transforms and a poster image] that kept the intent. The designer tweaked the motion, and I agreed to [keep the richer version for desktop].”

R: “We kept the visual idea, LCP stayed at [Y] s, and we shipped on time. We also agreed to review performance in design critique from then on.”

L: “I now bring a measurement or prototype early, so the conversation is about the user, not about who is right.”

  • With a backend developer (API shape, pagination, error format): agree a contract first (OpenAPI), mock it so both sides unblock, and name the cost of each shape for the client, such as an N+1 request count.
  • Never go around the person or complain to the manager before talking to them directly.
  • If you were wrong in the story, say so. It builds trust.

“Coordinating designers, backend engineers, QA and product”asked in 3 loops

Say this“I make dependencies explicit early and keep everyone unblocked: agree the API contract and a mock before backend is ready, review designs with the engineers before build, and give QA testable builds continuously, not at the end.”
  • Designers: design review before build, a shared component library and tokens, agreed states (loading, empty, error), and acceptance of “close enough” pixel differences.
  • Backend: contract-first (OpenAPI/types), mock server, integration test environment, versioning plan.
  • QA: acceptance criteria written with product, preview deployments per PR, automated E2E for critical flows, clear bug reports with steps.
  • Product: a visible plan, trade-off conversations in terms of user and business impact, and early demos.

“A client or stakeholder has unrealistic expectations”asked in 3 loops

Say this“I do not say ‘no’ first. I find the goal behind the request, show what it costs, and offer options: reduce scope, move the date, or add capacity, with the risk of each. Expectations are managed by being specific and early, not by being agreeable.”
sample answer (template)

“A stakeholder asked for [a large feature] in [too short a time]. I asked what outcome they needed by that date, which turned out to be [a demo for a customer]. I broke the request into [what a demo needs] and [what production needs], estimated each with a range, and presented three options: (1) demo-quality in [N days], (2) full feature in [M weeks], (3) full feature faster with [one more engineer / reduced scope], listing the risks. They chose option [1] first, and we did [2] afterwards. They later said [a positive outcome].”

  • Give ranges (best, likely, worst), not a single optimistic date.
  • Put it in writing afterwards. Surprises later usually come from verbal agreements.

Remember!

  • Goal first, then evidence, then options
  • Prototype or measure instead of arguing
  • Decision owner decides, then commit
  • Offer options, not just “no”

“Explain performance metrics (TTI, FCP, CLS) to non-technical stakeholders”asked in 3 loops

Say this“I translate each metric into a user moment and a business outcome, give one number with a comparison, and end with a decision I need from them. No jargon unless they ask.”
MetricSay it like thisLink to the business
FCP / LCP“How long until the page looks ready. Today it is [4.1] seconds; our target is under [2.5].”visitors leave slow pages before they see the product
TTI / INP“How quickly it reacts when someone taps or types. Right now [the filter button] feels stuck for [0.6] seconds.”frustrated users abandon forms and carts
CLS“Whether the page jumps around while you read. It makes people tap the wrong thing.”mis-clicks, support tickets, lower trust
sample answer (template)

“I use three parts. One: the user experience in plain words, ‘on a mid-range phone, the product page took [N] seconds to show the price.’ Two: why it matters to us: ‘[a similar industry study / our own analytics] show conversion drops as load time rises; our [mobile checkout] is where we lose the most.’ Three: the plan and the ask: ‘We can bring it to [M] seconds in [two sprints] by [compressing images and removing unused code]. I need [one sprint of capacity] and will report the number weekly.’ After we shipped, I showed a before/after chart.”

  • Use analogies sparingly: a restaurant (“the menu appears fast but the food is slow”) can help, but do not stretch it.
  • Visuals: a side-by-side filmstrip or video of the page loading is more persuasive than a chart.
  • Always give a comparison (before and after, competitor, target), and say how confident you are.

“Explain the architecture of a frontend-heavy app you built”asked in 3 loops

Say this“I walk through it top-down in about four minutes: what it does and for whom, the shape (rendering strategy, folders, state), the two or three decisions that mattered with their alternatives, and what I would change. Then I ask which area they would like to go deeper on.”
1. PURPOSE (20 s)   What it is, users, scale (users, pages, team size), my role
2. SHAPE (60 s)     Stack + rendering (CSR/SSR), routing, state split (server vs client state),
                    API layer, auth, design system, testing, deployment
3. DECISIONS (90 s) For 2 or 3 choices: "I chose X over Y because Z; the cost was W"
                    e.g. TanStack Query vs Redux for server data; SSR vs CSR for SEO;
                    feature-based folders vs layer-based
4. HARD PART (45 s) The most difficult problem and how it was solved (performance, offline, real time)
5. RESULT (20 s)    Metric: load time, error rate, adoption, team velocity
6. HINDSIGHT (20 s) What I would change with what I know now
  • Draw while you talk. A simple box diagram (browser → API → database, plus cache and auth) helps both of you.
  • Know the numbers: bundle size, number of routes, requests per page, test coverage, deploy frequency.
  • “Proof of quality” (asked for senior roles): tests and coverage at the right level, CI gates, error and performance monitoring, accessibility checks, code review standards, and incident history. Bring one concrete example of each you actually did.

“Which of your projects are you most proud of?”asked in 3 loops

sample answer (template)

“I am proudest of [project] because [the impact: users, revenue, time saved] and because of how it was done: [a specific engineering decision, e.g. we moved to a design system, cutting new-screen build time from [X] days to [Y]]. My part was [what I personally led or built]. What I learned was [a lesson].”

⚠ weak answerNaming a project and listing technologies, with no result and no personal contribution. Interviewers want evidence of impact and judgement, not a stack list.

Remember!

  • User moment + business outcome + ask
  • Walkthrough: purpose → shape → decisions → hard part → result → hindsight
  • Draw it; know your numbers

“How do you mentor junior engineers?”asked in 4 loops

Say this“I try to teach how to think, not hand out answers. I set a clear goal with each person, give them work slightly beyond their comfort zone with a safety net, and give feedback that is specific, kind and timely. Success is when they no longer need me for that class of problem.”
  • Pairing and thinking aloud: debug together, narrate how I narrow a problem, then swap and let them drive.
  • Code review as teaching: explain the why, link to a doc or ADR, separate must-fix from nit, and praise what is good.
  • Stretch work: hand over a small feature end to end with a checkpoint at the design stage, not at the end.
  • Regular 1:1s: goals, blockers, feedback both ways.
  • Scale it: write down recurring advice (a team guide, example PRs), run a short knowledge-share.
sample answer (template)

“[Name], a [junior engineer], kept shipping components that re-rendered too much and was frustrated by review comments. I asked what they found hardest, and it was reading the Profiler. We paired for [an hour], I let them drive, and I only asked questions like ‘what changed between these renders?’. They then fixed [a real slow screen] themselves. Over [3 months] their PRs needed [fewer review rounds, from N to M], and they later [mentored a new joiner / led a feature]. What I took from it: asking the right question helps more than giving the answer.”

“How do you make and communicate a technical decision?”asked in 3 loops

Say this“I frame the problem and the constraints, list two or three real options with trade-offs, prototype only the riskiest unknown, write a short RFC or ADR, time-box the discussion, decide, and record why. Reversible decisions I make quickly; hard-to-reverse ones get more input.”
StepOutput
1 · Problem and constraintswhat we are solving, success criteria, deadlines, team skills
2 · Options2 to 3 candidates, including “do nothing”, each with cost, risk, reversibility
3 · Evidencea spike, benchmark or reference from another team
4 · Write it downADR: context, decision, alternatives, consequences
5 · Decide and commita named decision owner; “disagree and commit” for the rest
6 · Revisita review date or trigger (“if build times pass 10 min, reconsider”)
sample answer (template)

“We needed to choose [a state library / a micro-frontend approach / SSR vs CSR] for [project]. I wrote a one-page ADR comparing [A, B, C] on [the criteria that mattered: team familiarity, bundle size, testability]. I ran a [two-day] spike on the riskiest part. We held a [30-minute] review, and the team chose [B]; two engineers preferred [A] and I made sure their concerns were recorded as risks with a trigger to revisit. After [6 months] [the outcome and what we would adjust].”

“How do you raise standards or introduce a change across a team?”asked in 3 loops

  • Start from a pain the team already feels (flaky tests, slow reviews, repeated bugs), not from a preference.
  • Pilot small: one team or module, measure, then expand. Show results rather than mandating.
  • Automate it: a lint rule, template or CI check beats a wiki page nobody reads.
  • Bring people along: ask for objections early, credit others, make the old way easy to leave.
  • Leave a trail: a short guide, an ADR and an example PR.

“A teammate’s work is below standard, or a colleague is struggling”asked in 3 loops

Say this“I talk to them privately and early, describe the specific behaviour or outcome (not the person), ask what is getting in the way, and agree what support and what change looks like. I involve the manager only if it continues, and I tell them I am doing so.”
  • Ask first: unclear requirements, personal circumstances, a skills gap and a motivation problem each need a different response.
  • Offer something concrete: pairing, a smaller task, a code walkthrough, better documentation.
  • Protect the delivery: adjust who does what while the support takes effect.

Remember!

  • Mentor: teach thinking, specific feedback, stretch with a net
  • Decide: options → evidence → ADR → commit → revisit
  • Standards: pain first, pilot, automate
  • Struggling teammate: private, specific, supportive

“Tell me about a mistake or failure”asked in 4 loops

Say this“I pick a real, mid-sized mistake that I owned, say plainly what I did wrong, what it cost, how I fixed it, and the process change that came from it. The lesson must be something that visibly changed how I work.”
sample answer (template)

S: “I [shipped a change without checking the older-browser / empty-state / failure case] on [project].”

A: “It caused [the impact: N users saw an error for M minutes]. I told [the manager and the team] immediately rather than hoping nobody noticed, helped [roll back / fix], and wrote the post-mortem, taking responsibility for [the specific decision].”

R + L: “Afterwards I [added a checklist item / a test / a staging check]. Since then [a concrete improvement: such issues have been caught before release].”

⚠ weak answers“My weakness is that I work too hard” or a failure that was really someone else’s fault. Also avoid a mistake so serious it raises doubts about judgement (data loss through carelessness) or so trivial it sounds evasive.

“Tell me about working with an ambiguous or changing requirement”asked in 3 loops

Say this“I reduce ambiguity quickly: ask the questions that change the design, write down assumptions and share them, build the smallest slice that tests the riskiest assumption, and make the system easy to change where I am least sure.”
  • List assumptions and get them confirmed (“I am assuming users can edit after submit. Correct?”).
  • Thin vertical slice to validate with the user early; avoid building the whole thing on a guess.
  • Keep it flexible: config instead of hard-coding, a feature flag, an adapter in front of an uncertain API.
  • Changing requirements: agree how change is handled (trade scope for time), and keep a visible change log.

“How do you take feedback, and how do you give hard feedback?”asked in 3 loops

  • Receiving: listen without defending, ask for an example, summarise it back, decide what to change, and follow up later to show you acted. Give a real example where feedback changed your behaviour.
  • Giving: be timely, specific and about observable behaviour; say the impact; ask for their view; agree a next step. “In the review yesterday, the PR had no tests for [X], which caused [Y] last month. What would help you add them?”
  • Code review disagreement: separate preference from correctness, link to a standard, and defer to the author on style that does not matter.

Quick checkanswers folded away

1. What does STAR(L) stand for, and where should most of the time go?

Situation, Task, Action, Result, Lesson. About half on the Action: what you did and why.

2. Why prepare a story bank instead of one answer per question?

Six to eight real stories can answer dozens of questions by changing the emphasis, and you can discuss them in depth when probed.

3. In a production incident, what comes first: finding the root cause or restoring service?

Restoring service (rollback or flag off). Root cause comes after, then a blameless post-mortem with concrete prevention actions.

4. How do you handle a disagreement with a designer?

Understand their goal, restate the shared goal, bring a prototype or measurement, offer options with trade-offs, let the decision owner decide, then commit.

5. How do you explain CLS to a non-technical stakeholder?

“Whether the page jumps while you read, which makes people tap the wrong thing,” then the business effect and the plan.

6. What is “disagree and commit”?

You state your concern, the owner decides, and afterwards you fully support the decision. The concern is recorded, with a trigger to revisit if it proves right.

7. What makes a good “mistake” story?

A real, mid-sized mistake you owned, its cost, how you fixed it, and a process change you can point to afterwards.

8. How do you handle an unrealistic request?

Find the goal behind it, estimate with ranges, and present options (reduce scope, move the date, add capacity) with the risk of each.

9. “We” or “I”?

Credit the team once, then describe your own actions with “I”, because you are the one being evaluated.

10. How long should an answer be?

About two minutes. Stop and let the interviewer ask follow-ups.
